The imaginary number is $i$, and $i$ is defined as,
$$
i=\sqrt{-1}
$$
Classically, the square root function only has a meaningful answer for positive numbers: it is the inverse function of the exponential function, or squaring, that is, it *undoes* the exponential operation. Classically any negative number squared yields a positive product, making it a one-way operation.

Mathematicians, however, in the spirit of naming concepts even before they're properly defined, began working with square roots of negative numbers and eventually named $\sqrt{-1}$ as $i$.

# Naming
Descartes called these numbers "imaginary" as an insult. Gauss, more properly, wanted to rename all the kinds of numbers from the confusing "positive", "negative", and "imaginary", to the more intuitive "direct", "inverse", and "lateral". Gasses also created the term "complex number" and "complex plane", and sadly those are the only terms which persist today.
# History & Development
Imaginary numbers were first observed in the wild by the estimable [[Girolamo Cardano]] in his work *Ars Magna*, which covered the roots of cubic functions. He was forced to encounter square roots of negative numbers since a cubic formula is guaranteed to always have at least one point where it touches the x-axis. But Cardano considered the square root expressions meaningless and junk.
