The term "skin effect" has two meaning in two different contexts: AC currents and electromagnetic waves.

# AC Current Skin Effect

# EM Wave Skin Effect
When an oscillating electromagnetic wave (or truly any electromagnetic field) is applied across a conductive material, the skin effect occurs. The conductor is filled with charged particles which can move freely or relatively freely through the conductor. When an electric or magnetic field is applied across these charges, it induces the charges to move, and some portion of the field's energy will be expended in causing each charged particle to move. For that reason, an electromagnetic field applied across the conductive material will expend all of its energy in moving the free charges inside the conductor. For every unit of distance that the wave travels inside the conductor material, the field strength will attenuate in proportion to how many charged particles are present in the affected area. On a macro-scale, we determine the rate of attenuation using the material's [[Conductivity|conductivity]], which is a macro-scale representation of how many free charges are present and how freely they can move.

If charges have been moved by the energy of some incident EM wave, there will then be a charge displacement on the surface of and within the conductor. A charge displacement induces an electromagnetic field (or wave!) of its own. For example, an incident electric field oriented in the $+z$ direction will move electrons to the $-z$ direction, thus creating a new electric field pointing in the $-z$ direction. If the incident wave oscillates at some frequency $f_{0}$, and creates a charge dislacement which oscillates back and forth at a frequency of $f_{0}$, then a new electromagnetic wave will be generated on the surface of the conductor, with a frequency of $f_{0}$ and an amplitude proportional to that of the incident wave but in the opposite direction. This is the mechanical reason for a [[Reflections and Refractions|wave reflection]].
